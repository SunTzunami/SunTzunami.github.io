SpendSight circa 2026

On-Device AI · Small LLMs · Evaluation · Personal Finance

TLDR:
Question: Can sub-2B LLMs handle personal finance Q&A?
Finding: Mostly yes - One of the best setups reached 89.1% task accuracy in ~3s using 1.2 GB RAM.

Back story:
I log my daily expenses in an excel and have custom dashboards, but they can’t answer ad-hoc questions like “how much did I spend on snacks over the past year?” or “compare my spending on groceries for jan 2026 vs jan 2025”.

I wondered: can I get sub-2B local LLMs to answer these reliably?

Why local? - I didn’t wanna share my personal finance data with cloud providers.
Why sub-2B? - I wanted quick responses (in <10s, ideally <5s) on my rather old PC (8GB M1 Mac).

I started experimenting with the top tiny LLMs (as per Artificial Analysis). Early experiments showed that they struggled to generate accurate analytics code. Tool calling seemed to work much better, but inserting every tool definition in the prompt hurt both speed and accuracy. So we split the task into tool selection and parameter generation.

We built a 115-query test suite and benchmarked six small LLMs on accuracy, speed, memory use and failure modes across two architectures: single-agent and dual-agent, on an 8GB M1 Mac.

The part I'm probably most proud of is that we did the whole thing independently, without funding or special infrastructure, and still managed to get the work accepted at workshops at COLM and EMNLP.

Also, on a completely different metric, the system helped me cut my snack spending by about 85%, saving more than ¥30,000 (and also a few body-fat % points, I guess).

SpendSight executing a local query and generating the spending trend chart in 3.4 seconds on an M1 Mac.

Do Leg Days Suck? circa 2024

Self-Experiment · Fitness · Data

I’d always heard my friends complain about leg days being torturous and I’d wonder if it’s actually that bad. I was a runner so I didn’t train legs until a few injuries forced me to start adding exercises like Bulgarian split squats to my routine.

Leg days were quite tough indeed. To check if there was a quantitative proof of leg days being challenging (average heart rate for the session served as a proxy to how difficult the session was), I thought of comparing my average heart rate during leg-day gym sessions with my average heart rate during other gym sessions. There wasn't a sophisticated statistical model behind it. I just compared the two series and performed a difference in means test.

The answer was pretty much what I wanted it to be:
Leg days are harder (or so the data tells).

I wonder how much of it is psychological with me dreading the next set of squat or split squat and heart rate rising in anticipation.

Avg HR on upper body weight training days vs lower body weight training days.

Is Exercising Good for One's Heart? circa 2020

Statistics · Self-Experiment · Health Data

“The important thing is not to stop questioning.”

- Albert Einstein

Back in my third semester at IITR (summer of 2020), my dad had gifted me a Fitbit at a time when fitness trackers were still a rarity in India. I had watched Hajime no Ippo around the same time and got inspired to start running. I started running almost daily and tracking my health metrics with the Fitbit.

After a few months of consistent running, I observed that my resting heart rate (RHR) had decreased from about mid 60s to late 50s. I read online that it's a sign of improving cardiovascular health and that RHR could be segregated into different ranges (60s being normal, late-50s being athletic and so on....), I was stoked to see that I'd moved into the slightly healthier band! But I wanted to be certain and in order to test this hypothesis, I extracted exercise data from my Fitbit records and evaluated its relationship with RHR by writing python scripts to generate scatter plots and compute correlation coefficients between rhr and cumulative exercise. Both of these indicated a clear negative trend and I was ecstatic to see quantified proof of improving cardiovascular health. Watching an everyday observation become a question that data could answer sparked my interest in applied data science.

It was a very small experiment, and in retrospect it was also statistically kinda naive as there were plenty of possible confounders, and an N=1 observational analysis certainly cannot establish causality.

I kept tracking my resting heart rate afterward too. During COVID, a sudden change in the metric helped alert me that something was wrong, and I self-isolated rather than risk passing it on to my family.

Space and Light circa 2018–2019

C++ · Computer Graphics · Astronomy · First Project

In 2018-19, when I was 17ish, I absolutely loathed programming classes.

We were using Turbo C++ (which I believe was the rage back in the Cretaceous), and programming felt frustratingly abstract to me. I couldn't really see what any of it was useful for.

Then our teacher asked us to make a project.

I liked math and science, and I was preparing for university entrance exams (which were math and science), so I thought: why not make something related to science?

I found the graphics library, started experimenting, and ended up building an animation that rendered stars and the Milky Way.

For the first time, the things we'd been learning in class (pointers, classes, abstraction, and all the rest) suddenly became useful parts of something I could actually see.

I got so engrossed in the project that I'd spend my lunch breaks in the computer lab, stuck to computer screen, trying to debug cpp code on ancient 80s era compilers, and ended up spending the entire holidays working on it.

My teacher was so surprised by the final result that she initially didn't believe I'd made it myself (as I was not exactly known for being a fan of programming back then).

That project completely changed how I thought about programming.

I also built a small quiz with a dynamic timing system, where faster answers earned more points, along with a leaderboard.

In retrospect, this project to me was what I presume LLMs are for most people (especially non-CS people), ability to communicate with a machine in written language and getting it to do what you want it to do - It felt magical!
